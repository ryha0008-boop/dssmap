TheDead Sea

Roughly a thousand manuscripts, copied between about 250 BCE and 135 CE and hidden in caves above the Dead Sea. Most were found between 1947 and 1956, and they are still being studied today.

The two halves of this page echo the Shrine of the Book in Jerusalem, where a black basalt wall faces a white dome: the "Sons of Darkness" and "Sons of Light" of the War Scroll.

What they are

The Dead Sea Scrolls are ancient Jewish manuscripts found in caves in the Judaean Desert, on the north-western shore of the Dead Sea. In the narrow sense the name refers to the scrolls from eleven caves near the ruins of Khirbet Qumran. More broadly it also covers manuscripts from other desert sites, such as Masada, Wadi Murabba'at and Nahal Hever.

The Qumran scrolls were copied from about the mid-3rd century BCE to the 1st century CE. They were probably hidden around the time the Romans destroyed the settlement at Qumran, during the Jewish revolt of 66–73 CE. The material from the other sites runs later, into the Bar Kokhba revolt of 132–135 CE.

Almost all of them survive only as fragments. A few are long and well preserved, like the Great Isaiah Scroll, but most are scraps, some no bigger than a fingernail. Scholars have sorted tens of thousands of fragments into roughly 950–1,000 separate manuscripts.

Before they were found, the oldest complete Hebrew Bible manuscripts dated to around 1000 CE. The scrolls are about a thousand years older. They showed both how carefully the biblical text had been copied, and that several different versions of some books were in circulation before the text was standardised.

How they were found

  1. 1946–47

    Bedouin shepherds of the Ta'amireh tribe, Muhammad edh-Dhib and his cousins, find jars holding seven scrolls in a cave (later Cave 1) near Qumran.

  2. 1947

    The scrolls pass through a Bethlehem antiquities dealer, Khalil Iskander Shahin ("Kando"). Eleazar Sukenik of the Hebrew University buys three (Isaiah b, War Scroll, Thanksgiving Hymns). Mar Athanasius Samuel, the Syrian Orthodox metropolitan, buys the other four.

  3. 1949

    Archaeologists Gerald Lankester Harding and Roland de Vaux excavate Cave 1 and recover hundreds more fragments.

  4. 1951–56

    De Vaux excavates the ruins of Khirbet Qumran itself, finding a settlement with cisterns, ritual baths, a large hall and a cemetery.

  5. 1952

    Caves 2 to 6 are found. Cave 4, discovered by Bedouin, holds tens of thousands of fragments from more than 500 manuscripts, by far the largest find. Cave 3 yields the Copper Scroll. Wadi Murabba'at caves are explored the same year.

  6. 1954

    Mar Samuel advertises his four scrolls in the Wall Street Journal. Yigael Yadin, Sukenik's son, buys them through an intermediary for $250,000, reuniting all seven Cave 1 scrolls in Israel.

  7. 1955–56

    Caves 7 to 10 are found in the marl terrace near the site. Cave 11 is found by Bedouin in 1956 and yields the Temple Scroll, the Great Psalms Scroll and the Targum of Job.

  8. 1960–61

    Yadin's expeditions to Nahal Hever (the Cave of Letters) find Bar Kokhba's letters and the Babatha archive.

  9. 1963–65

    Yadin's excavations at Masada find fragments of 14 scrolls left behind by the rebels.

  10. 1965

    The Shrine of the Book opens at the Israel Museum to house the Cave 1 scrolls.

  11. 1967

    After the Six-Day War, Yadin acquires the Temple Scroll from Kando, who had kept it hidden.

  12. 1991

    After decades of slow publication by a small team, the Huntington Library opens its photographs to all scholars, and a computer-reconstructed text is published, effectively ending the monopoly.

  13. 2009–11

    The official edition, Discoveries in the Judaean Desert (DJD), reaches 40 volumes and is essentially complete.

  14. 2011–12

    The Israel Museum puts its five Cave 1 scrolls online. The Israel Antiquities Authority launches the Leon Levy Dead Sea Scrolls Digital Library with multispectral photos of the fragments.

  15. 2017

    Archaeologists announce a twelfth Qumran cave holding broken jars and wrappings, but its scrolls had already been looted.

  16. 2018–20

    The Museum of the Bible in Washington announces that all 16 of its "Dead Sea Scroll" fragments are modern forgeries. Many fragments sold on the market after 2002 are now treated as suspect.

  17. 2021

    The Israel Antiquities Authority announces new fragments of the Greek Twelve Minor Prophets (Zechariah and Nahum) from the "Cave of Horror" in Nahal Hever, the first new scroll fragments found in about 60 years.

  18. 2025

    A study combining radiocarbon dates with a handwriting-analysis AI model called "Enoch" suggests that several scrolls are older than their handwriting had previously suggested.

What the scrolls contain

Scholars usually sort the material into a few broad groups. The categories in the catalogue follow the same lines.

Biblical manuscripts

Copies of the books of the Hebrew Bible make up roughly a quarter of the Qumran manuscripts. Every book is represented except Esther (Nehemiah survives only as part of Ezra-Nehemiah, if at all). The most-copied books were Psalms (about 36 copies), Deuteronomy (about 30) and Isaiah (about 21), the same books most often quoted in the New Testament. Some copies follow the text later standardised by the Masoretes. Others match the Hebrew behind the Greek Septuagint, or the expanded text of the Samaritan Pentateuch.

Tefillin and mezuzot

Tiny parchment slips with passages from Exodus and Deuteronomy, once folded into leather phylactery cases or doorpost containers. A few were found still in their cases.

Other religious writings

Works known before 1947 only in translation, like Jubilees, Enoch, Tobit and Ben Sira, now appeared in their original Hebrew or Aramaic. Others were entirely new: retellings of biblical stories (the Genesis Apocryphon), commentaries (pesharim) applying prophecy to current events, hymns, liturgies, wisdom texts, calendars and visions of a new Jerusalem.

Sectarian writings

A smaller group describes a particular community, the Yahad. Its rule books (the Community Rule, the Damascus Document), its hymns and its end-time war plan reveal a priestly group at odds with the Jerusalem temple leadership, led by a "Teacher of Righteousness". Most scholars connect it with the Essenes described by Josephus, Philo and Pliny, though that link is still debated.

Documents

Mostly from sites other than Qumran: letters, contracts, deeds and accounts, including the personal archive of a woman named Babatha and letters from Simon bar Kosiba (Bar Kokhba), leader of the second revolt against Rome.

The major scrolls

These are the best-known and best-preserved manuscripts. Each links to its full text in the catalogue and to its high-resolution images.

1QIsaᵃ

The Great Isaiah Scroll

The only biblical book among the scrolls that survives complete, all 66 chapters on 17 sheets of parchment sewn together. Its text is very close to the medieval Masoretic text, with thousands of small differences in spelling and some real variant readings. It is the most famous and most photographed of the scrolls.

1QS

The Community Rule

A handbook for the Yahad community: how to join, a year-long probation, communal meals, penalties for misbehaviour (from interrupting a speaker to falling asleep in the assembly), and a teaching about two spirits, of truth and of falsehood, at war in every person. The Rule of the Congregation (1QSa) and the Rule of Blessings (1QSb) were attached to the same scroll.

1QpHab

The Habakkuk Commentary (Pesher)

Quotes Habakkuk a few lines at a time, then explains what each passage "really" refers to: the community's own leader, the Teacher of Righteousness, his enemy the Wicked Priest, and the invading Kittim, usually taken to be the Romans. The divine name is written in the old Paleo-Hebrew script, standing out from the square letters around it.

1QM

The War Scroll

A detailed plan for a 40-year final war between the "Sons of Light" and the "Sons of Darkness", including battle formations, inscriptions for trumpets and banners, weapons and the prayers of the priests. The black wall and white dome of the Shrine of the Book were designed around its imagery.

1QHᵃ

The Thanksgiving Hymns (Hodayot)

Around 30 hymns that each begin "I thank you, Lord". Some speak in the voice of a persecuted leader, perhaps the Teacher of Righteousness himself. Others express the worthlessness of human beings before God and their joy at being given knowledge.

1QapGen

The Genesis Apocryphon

A retelling of Genesis in Aramaic, from Lamech and the birth of Noah to Abraham. It adds vivid details the Bible leaves out, such as a long description of Sarah's beauty. It was so brittle that it could not be unrolled until 1956, and parts were read only with infrared photography decades later.

3Q15

The Copper Scroll

Unlike every other scroll, it is engraved on metal and is not a literary text. It lists about 64 hiding places of gold, silver and sacred vessels, with directions like "in the cistern below the rampart". It was so corroded that in 1955–56 it had to be cut into strips with a saw before it could be read. None of the treasure has ever been found.

11QTᵃ

The Temple Scroll

Presented as God speaking to Moses in the first person, it gives a plan for an enormous ideal temple, along with laws for festivals, sacrifices, purity and the conduct of the king. Kando hid it in a shoebox under his floor for over ten years before Yigael Yadin acquired it in 1967.

11QPsᵃ

The Great Psalms Scroll

Contains about 40 biblical psalms in an order unlike the standard Book of Psalms, mixed with poems not in the Hebrew Bible, including Psalm 151 (known before only in Greek and Syriac). A prose note claims that David wrote 3,600 psalms and 450 songs.

4QMMT

Some Works of the Torah (MMT)

A letter listing about 20 points of religious law where "we" disagree with how the Jerusalem temple is run, on purity, sacrifices and marriage, and urging "you" to accept "our" interpretation. Many of the rulings match positions later attributed to the Sadducees. Its publication was delayed for decades and became the focus of a famous lawsuit.

CD / 4QD

The Damascus Document

Two medieval copies turned up in a Cairo synagogue store-room half a century before Qumran, and nobody knew where the text came from. The Qumran copies proved it was a work of the same movement. It tells of a group that went into exile in the "land of Damascus" and lays out its rules for members living in towns, with wives and children.

Read them as a book

A companion reading edition gathers everything legible into 37 chapters by subject: the Bible as the scrolls preserve it, including the Greek scrolls, the stories of Enoch, Noah and Abraham, the Community's rules, prayers, wisdom and visions of the end, and the letters and family papers that refugees of the Bar Kokhba revolt hid in nearby caves. Each chapter is set beside passages from the Hebrew Bible, the New Testament, the Quran, the Talmud, the Ethiopian Bible, Zoroastrian scripture, and Hindu and Buddhist texts. The complete 1 Enoch and Jubilees are included.

Where to see the photos

Almost every fragment has been photographed, and the main archives are free to use online.

Leon Levy Dead Sea Scrolls Digital Library

The Israel Antiquities Authority's archive. Multispectral and infrared images of nearly every fragment, plus the historic 1950s photographs. The "Photos" links in the catalogue page go here.

The Digital Dead Sea Scrolls (Israel Museum)

Very high-resolution, zoomable photos of the Great Isaiah Scroll, Community Rule, War Scroll, Temple Scroll and Habakkuk Commentary, with an English translation of Isaiah.

Shrine of the Book, Jerusalem

Where the Cave 1 scrolls and the Temple Scroll are kept. A facsimile of the Isaiah scroll is on permanent display; originals rotate for conservation.

The Jordan Museum, Amman

Holds the Copper Scroll and other fragments that were in Jordanian custody before 1967.
